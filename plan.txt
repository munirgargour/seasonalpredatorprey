Abstract
Ecological systems exhibit variability driven by seasonal environ￾mental changes that affect resource availability and metabolic rates.
This project develops a seasonal agent-based simulation of fox￾rabbit interactions that cycles through spring, summer, fall, and
winter, each imposing different constraints on population dynamics.
Unlike classical predator-prey models that assume constant envi￾ronmental conditions, this implementation incorporates seasonal
modifiers that affect metabolism rates (+ or - 10%), and grass regen￾eration (+ or - 10%). Prey agents use hunger-based food-seeking
behavior which activates only when their energy reserves fall below
90% capacity, preventing overfeeding and preserving food stores.
The Python-based simulation framework enables investigation of
how seasonal fluctuations influence population stability, extinction
risk, and the amplitude of predator-prey oscillations across annual
cycles.
1 Introduction
Natural ecosystems exist in dynamic environments where seasonal
changes create fluctuations in resource availability and metabolic
rates. These seasonal variables add complexity beyond classical
predator-prey theory, which typically assumes constant environ￾mental parameters. Understanding how populations respond to
seasonal changes is important to our understanding of how these
systems work in the real world.
This project addresses four primary research questions: (1) How
do seasonal cycles affect the amplitude and period of predator-prey
oscillations? (2) What effect does the seasonal model have on popu￾lation stability compared to a consistent-environment baseline? (3)
What degree of environmental effect (amplitude of grass regrowth
and metabolic rate effects) stabilize agent populations best?
We develop an agent-based computational model where au￾tonomous rabbit and fox agents interact in a 2D environment sub￾ject to four-season annual cycles. Spring enhances plant growth,
summer reduces metabolic costs, fall maintains baseline conditions,
and winter increases energy demands and reduces food produc￾tion. Prey conserve food supply by suppressing food-seeking when
hunger exceeds 10 percent (energy < 90), while predators patrol
and employ vision-based hunting strategies.
2 Theoretical Background
2.1 Classical Predator-Prey Theory
The Lotka-Volterra equations [1, 3] provide the foundational math￾ematical description of predator-prey dynamics:
𝑑𝑁prey
𝑑𝑡 = 𝑟𝑁prey − 𝑎𝑁prey𝑁pred (1)
𝑑𝑁pred
𝑑𝑡 = 𝑏𝑎𝑁prey𝑁pred − 𝑚𝑁pred (2)
where 𝑟 is prey intrinsic growth, 𝑎 is attack rate, 𝑏 is conversion
efficiency, and𝑚 is predator mortality. This system generates stable
cycles with a constant amplitude determined by initial conditions.
However, this model assumes constant parameters, which are
not present in nature. Seasonal temperature fluctuations affect meta￾bolic rates, and resource availability varies with growing seasons.
2.2 Seasonal Extensions
Incorporating seasonal changes changes autonomous differential
equations into non-autonomous systems. Mathematical analysis
from Murray's text [2] provides a detailed seasonal forcing analysis showing that seasonal changes can stabilize or destabilize population
dynamics depending on specific amplitude-stability relationships [2].
We can implement seasonal effects through multiplicative modi￾fiers:
𝑑𝑁prey
𝑑𝑡 = 𝑟(𝑡)𝑁prey − 𝑎(𝑡)𝑁prey𝑁pred (3)
where 𝑟(𝑡) and 𝑎(𝑡) vary periodically with season. The approxi￾mation operationalizes this through season-dependent metabolism
and resource regeneration rates. Specifically, 𝑟(𝑡) = 𝑟_base × seasonal_modifier[current_season] where seasonal_modifier = {SPRING: 1.1, SUMMER: 1.0, FALL: 1.0, WINTER: 0.9} for grass growth, and similarly metabolism_multiplier = {SPRING: 1.0, SUMMER: 0.9, FALL: 1.0, WINTER: 1.1}.
2.3 Energy Budget Framework
Following the ecological energetics principles found in [2], we can
model agents as energy reservoirs subject to:
𝑑𝐸
𝑑𝑡 = 𝐸intake − 𝐸metabolism − 𝐸activity (4)
Seasonal modifiers directly affect 𝐸metabolism, with winter increas￾ing costs by 10% (body temperature regulation demands) and sum￾mer reducing costs by 10% (reduced demands). Grass regeneration,
determining 𝐸intake for rabbits, increases 10% in spring (growing
season) and decreases 10% in winter (dormancy).
This creates a more realistic annual energy cycle where pop￾ulations must survive winter bottlenecks using food stores from
previous seasons. Various amplitudes will be tested to simulate
different environments where, for example, winters may be harsher
or springs more productive.
2.4 Optimized Foraging and Hunger Thresholds
Optimal foraging theory says that animals should balance energy
gain against search costs. Our hunger-driven foraging implements

a simplified version of this principle: rabbits with >90% energy
reserves (hunger <10%) do not actively seek food, conserving food
stores for later use. This rate can be changed to test its effect on
population balance.
This creates a two-mode behavioral strategy:
• Satiated mode (E > 90% max): Random walk
• Foraging mode (E <= 90% max): Active food search within
detection radius
This behavioral switching will increase food abundance by pre￾venting satiated rabbits from consuming the food supply and con￾sequently starving.
3 System Architecture
3.1 Environmental Framework
The simulation environment is a bounded continuous domain of
500 x 500 units with toroidal boundary conditions, maintaining:
• Collections of active rabbit and fox agents
• Current season state 𝑆 ∈ {SPRING, SUMMER, FALL, WINTER}
• Season counter tracking timesteps within current season
Seasons cycle with period 𝑇season (can be adjusted, default 250
timesteps). Each season applies specific modifiers to environmental
processes.

4 Simulation Mechanics
4.1 Timesteps
The simulation advances in discrete timesteps Δ𝑡 = 1. Each cycle
processes:
(1) Season management: Check if season transition required
(every 250 steps)
(2) Rabbit updates: Process all living rabbits sequentially
(3) Fox updates: Process all living foxes sequentially
(4) Grass regeneration: Update resource levels based on growth
rate using new_grass_per_timestep = base_spawn_rate × seasonal_grass_modifier, where base_spawn_rate = 5 (spawn randomly across map)
(5) Agent cleanup: Remove deceased agents from collections
(6) Data recording: Log population and energy statistics
4.2 Spatial Queries
Efficient neighbor searches use KD-tree spatial indexing (using
SciPy, via scipy.spatial.KDTree.query_ball_point(position, radius)). For each predator perception check, we can query rabbits
within radius 𝑅𝑣 , resulting in 𝑂(log 𝑁) lookup complexity versus
𝑂(𝑁) for brute force.
4.3 Random Elements
Multiple sources of randomness prevent deterministic artifacts:
• Reproduction success: Reproduction can fail, resulting in
expended energy.
• Hunt outcomes: Possibility of failed hunt can cause predators
to expend more energy during a hunt
• Movement: Random walk directions from [0, 2𝜋)
• Initial conditions: Agent positions and energies randomized
within a range
This randomness, combined with seasonal effects, generates
complex population trajectories.
5 Literature Foundation
5.1 Population Dynamics Theory
Classical predator-prey dynamics come from Lotka [1] studying
chemical oscillations and Volterra [3] analyzing Adriatic fishery
data. Their differential equations revealed that predator-prey sys￾tems naturally oscillate with predators lagging prey by one-quarter
cycle, showing that increased predation leads to predator decline
by depleting the prey base.
5.2 Agent-Based Modeling Precedents
Wilensky’s NetLogo Wolf-Sheep Predation model [4] pioneered
agent-based approaches to predator-prey systems for education
and research. While our implementation is built from scratch in
Python rather than using frameworks, Wilensky’s design informs
several choices: grass regrowth mechanics (including concrete parameter values that we used for comparing our own grass regrowth rates), energy-based reproduction thresholds, and movement patterns.
We extend beyond basic agent-based models by incorporating:
(1) seasonal environmental variations, (2) hunger-driven behav￾ioral switching, (3) continuous space with vision-based pursuit
versus grid-based random encounters, and (4) comprehensive data
collection enabling quantitative answers to our predictions.

The simulation records metrics at every timestep:

8 Simplifying Assumptions
To prevent scope creep and keep the model within realistic param￾eters:
(1) Discrete Seasons: Abrupt transitions between seasons
(2) Homogeneous Space: No terrain features other than grass
(3) Asexual Reproduction: No mate-finding or sexual selec￾tion
(4) No Age Structure: All agents are adults equivalent in capa￾bilities
(5) Perfect Detection Within Range: Predators have complete
information about prey within vision radius
(6) Movement Speed: Speed constant rather than energy-dependent
(7) Fixed Season Length: Equal duration seasons (250 timesteps
each)
(8) No Disease or Parasites: Only starvation and predation as
mortality sources
(9) Single Prey, Single Predator: No competition or alternative
food sources
(10) Instantaneous Reproduction: No gestation periods
These simplifications focus the model on core seasonal and for￾aging dynamics.

11 Expected Outcomes
(1) Seasonal Population Cycles: Populations should exhibit
annual patterns with:
• Spring: Recovery and rapid growth as grass regenerates
• Summer: Peak populations with less starvation mortality,
and decreased population oscillation amplitude
• Fall: Gradual decline in population and return to normal
amplitudes
• Winter: Population drops due to elevated mortality
(2) Oscillation Amplitude: Compared to constant-environment
baselines, seasonal forcing should increase peak-to-trough
population variance considerably.
(3) Winter Bottleneck: Winter will be the limiting season. Pop￾ulations entering winter with insufficient energy reserves
will face extinction as starvation triggers both starvation
mortality and reduced reproduction from decrease in en￾ergy.
(4) Spatial Patterns: During winter, we expect clustering around
grass resources. During summer, populations should disperse
more uniformly due to hunter-based foraging mechanics.

13 Conclusion and Future Directions
This milestone establishes a foundation for a seasonally-explicit
agent-based predator-prey simulation incorporating environmental
variability and hunger-driven behavior. By combining seasonal
variation, hunger thresholds, and spatial structure, we create a
model capable of revealing how populations navigate fluctuating
environments more accurately than a simple equation.
Subsequent project phases will focus on:
• Implementation and adjustment of seasonal modifiers and
foraging logic
• Comparison against constant-environment baselines
• Multi-year simulation runs exploring long-term dynamics
• Statistical analysis of extinction probabilities under different
initial conditions
The Python framework enables easy experimentation while
maintaining reliable data collection for analysis. Results will show
how seasonal bottlenecks shape population sizes and hunger levels
in variable environments.
